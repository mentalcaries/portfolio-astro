Interviews are hard.

I make this statement as someone who's had to sit on both sides of the table. As an applicant, you have a short period of time, to present relevant aspects of yourself and possibly show some other personality traits that represent your whole self. As an interviewer, you have a short period of time assess and learn about the person to figure whether they _might_ be a good fit for the position.

For the most part, this has been an generally well-accepted process. There has to be some meeting, some interaction and some way to figure out if this a person I/we want to work with. We try to exchange enough information to give/formulate enough of an impression. And then we make a best guess (or a calculated guess) about which direction we take, or maybe move to another round of interviews to gather more information. Then we make a best guess again.

Enter the field of software engineering, where this process gets unnecessarily complex. It's quite common to have _multiple_ rounds of interviews: job fit, technical, second technical, HR, tech lead, CTO and maybe even CEO depending on the size of the company. You'd think that with such an elaborate process, it's already been figured out. But the reality is that they're still just making a best guess, albeit a _more informed_ one.

In my times of hiring for my business in clinical dentistry, I've often tried to find some way to correlate interview performance to actual job performance. After 10 years of doing that, I can safely say that I have not found any and here's why:

- interviewees want to create a good impression and put on their best face which can be genuine or a fabricated persona
- people can train and prepare for interviews by having pre-formed answers to known questions
- some people get really anxious and tense and just don't show their true abilities under pressure (trust me, _I'm one of them_).

These patterns don't seem limited to any field, as I see them again in tech where I am responsible for leading interviews and finding the right candidates for my team. And I'm no expert, but I have developed a much better instinct for who's going to be a better fit, even with these meetings conducted via video calls. Maybe some of that comes with getting older, or from previously working in a very personal, hands-on setting with high anxiety situations where you really had to read the individual.

Either way, for interviewers and interviewees alike, here are some of the things that have stood out to me:

- **Presentation:** this comprises many things, but I'd go so far to include things like personal appearance (including on-screen presence), punctuality, preparation and, in an online setting, video and audio quality. _How_ you show up does say a lot about you.
- **Enthusiasm:** do they have the right type of energy and zeal for the role? How much does the prospect of this work you? Does their passion shine through as they talk about related work/projects/experiences? Or are they coming across as dull and uninterested?
- **Padding and Fluff:** It's gotten easier to tell when candidates are just making things up or telling you what they think you want to hear. It's ok if you're not a good fit for _this_ position, but be true to yourself; trying to fake it just to get in the door doesn't always scale well.

One solution to this (for both parties involved) is **the working interview**, which is essentially short-term on the job experience (a week at most). From that, I can gauge more about the candidate - their work ethic, proactivity, initiative and willingness to learn - in the actual work environment with the team. There's still no guarantee in the long term, but it does give more information to work with.

As someone who works much better they can interview, I wish this is an approach more companies and hiring teams would take. Narrow it down to a few best guesses, and put them to work. In a real world setting, it's much easier to see who you want on your team.
